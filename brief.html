01
Bartz v. Anthropic Settled
Entertainment · Copyright · Authors class action · Approved September 2025
$1.5B
►

Anthropic downloaded over 7 million books from shadow libraries LibGen and PiLiMi to train its AI models. Three authors filed suit in August 2024. Judge Alsup certified a class of nearly 500,000 works. Statutory damages could have reached $70 billion. Settlement approved September 25, 2025 at approximately $3,000 per work, paid in four installments through September 2027. Final approval hearing May 14, 2026.

What was missing
No pre-ingestion consent record. No Training Receipt. No scope limitation. Once embedded in the AI algorithm, the exposure is permanent and inherits to every derivative model trained on those outputs, indefinitely.
What DDT changes
A Verification Receipt documents that consent was current before ingestion. Scope and duration are bounded. A breach is traceable and limited to defined works — not a permanent unbounded embedding across the entire model lineage.
02
State of Texas v. Meta Settled
Biometric · Facial geometry · Texas CUBI · First-ever enforcement · July 2024
$1.4B
►

Meta ran facial recognition software on virtually every face in photos uploaded to Facebook from 2011 onward, capturing facial geometry records of millions of Texans without written informed consent. This violated the Texas Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (CUBI). The first-ever CUBI enforcement action. Largest privacy settlement ever obtained by a single state attorney general. Paid over five years, announced July 30, 2024.

What was missing
No written consent at point of facial geometry capture. No audit trail. No record of what biometric data was collected, when, or for what purpose. CUBI requires all three before any collection begins.
What DDT changes
DDT's consent record documents written consent with scope and duration at the moment of capture. A live permission state query confirms authorization is current at the moment of use. Both records are cryptographically signed and timestamped.
03
State of Texas v. Google Settled
Biometric · Voiceprints & facial geometry · Texas CUBI + DTPA · Finalized October 2025
$1.375B
►

Texas sued Google for collecting voiceprints and facial geometry through Google Photos, Google Assistant, and Nest Hub Max without the written consent required by CUBI. Settlement announced May 9, 2025, finalized October 31, 2025. Largest single-state settlement ever against Google. Same statute, same mechanism as the Meta case — two billion-dollar outcomes in 16 months. Voiceprints are explicitly biometric identifiers under CUBI.

What was missing
No consent record for voiceprint or facial geometry capture via consumer products. Voice biometric collection for AI training was never disclosed as a use case separate from device functionality.
What DDT changes
AI training is a separately governed scope requiring its own consent record with expiry, authorized use cases, and duration — independent from the original device consent. Every capture generates a documented, machine-verifiable audit entry.
04
Vacker v. ElevenLabs Settled
Entertainment · Voice · Right of publicity · DMCA circumvention · First of 48 active AI copyright cases · August 2025
Confidential
►

Voice actors Karissa Vacker and Mark Boyett sued ElevenLabs for training its AI voice synthesis platform on their audiobook recordings without consent, alleging right of publicity violations and DMCA circumvention. First settlement in any of the 48 active AI copyright cases filed against AI companies. Mediated August 18, 2025. Case officially closed November 2025. ElevenLabs had received $280M in funding at a $1–3B valuation. The risk of going to trial outweighed the cost of settling.

What was missing
No consent record separating audiobook narration rights from AI training rights. Consenting to narrate a book does not authorize using that recording to train a commercial AI voice product. Scope was never documented.
What DDT changes
Voice attribute consent is governed per use case. A consent record for audiobook narration explicitly does not authorize AI training. Training use requires a separate record with its own scope, duration, and compensation — legally distinct and machine-verifiable.
05
Clearview AI v. BIPA class Settled
Technology · Facial recognition · Illinois BIPA · 50 billion images · Final approval March 2025
$51.75M
►

Clearview scraped approximately 50 billion facial images from the internet to build a facial recognition database sold to law enforcement agencies, with no consent from any individual depicted. The potential class included virtually any person whose face had appeared online since 2017. Full statutory damages would have bankrupted the company. Settlement structured as a 23% equity stake — the first settlement of its kind. Final approval March 2025.

What was missing
No consent at collection. No disclosure to subjects. No limitation on downstream use or resale to law enforcement. BIPA requires written consent before any biometric collection regardless of whether images are publicly accessible online.
What DDT changes
Any system querying a face image database gets a live signed response: AUTHORIZED, UNVERIFIABLE, or SUSPENDED. If no consent record exists, the system receives UNVERIFIABLE and cannot proceed without liability. Fail-closed at all risk tiers.
06
Lehrman & Sage v. LOVO / Voiceverse (Bored Ape Yacht Club) Active
Gaming · NFT · Voice · Right of publicity · S.D.N.Y. · Proceeding July 2025
Pending
►

Voiceverse — a blockchain startup founded by Bored Ape Yacht Club — marketed AI voice cloning technology as purchasable and tradeable NFTs, promising buyers “unlimited and perpetual” commercial use rights. Parent company LOVO hired voice actors Paul Lehrman and Linnea Sage via Fiverr under false pretenses, telling them their recordings were for internal research only. Their voices became the default commercial voices in LOVO’s subscription service, sold as NFTs. Performers received $400–$1,200. Buyers received permanent commercial licenses to their voices. A federal judge allowed breach of contract, unfair business practices, and copyright infringement claims to proceed in July 2025.

What was missing
Consent was obtained under false scope — “internal research only.” The actual use was commercial AI training and NFT monetization at scale. Identity attributes became permanently tradeable financial instruments. No record governed authorized scope, duration, or use cases. Performers had no revocation mechanism.
What DDT changes
A scope constraint in the consent record limits use to the stated purpose. Any use outside that scope — AI training, NFT commercialization, commercial subscription — returns OUT_OF_SCOPE on a live query. The performer retains revocation rights at any time. Every derivative use creates a lineage record.

08
Bartz v. Anthropic Settled
AI training · Copyright · Books · N.D. Cal. · Settled 2025 — largest copyright settlement in U.S. history
$1.5B
►

Authors and rights-holders brought a class action alleging Anthropic downloaded approximately seven million pirated books to train its large language models without consent or payment. The settlement of $1.5 billion is the largest copyright settlement in U.S. history, covering roughly 500,000 copyrighted works at approximately $3,000 per work. The Northern District of California had previously ruled that Anthropic may have illegally downloaded the works; two months later the company settled rather than face potential statutory damages across the full corpus. Every AI developer training on web-sourced or third-party content faces the same structural exposure.

What was missing
No consent record existed for any of the roughly 500,000 works ingested. There was no mechanism to query whether a rights-holder had authorized training use at the moment of ingestion. The absence of a pre-ingestion consent record made the entire training corpus legally indefensible against a statutory damages claim.
What DDT changes
A DDT consent record on each work establishes, at the moment of ingestion, whether training use is authorized. A Verification Receipt provides a timestamped, signed record that the system queried authorization and received a response before ingesting. That record converts "did you have permission" from an unanswerable question into a documented, auditable fact.

Pending

Filed April 3, 2026, this class action alleges Apple secretly downloaded millions of copyrighted YouTube videos to train a generative AI video model, deploying automated tools to bypass platform security, rotating IP addresses through virtual machines, and distributing requests across machines to avoid detection. The same plaintiffs filed parallel suits against Meta, Nvidia, ByteDance, and Snap over related AI training practices. YouTube's CEO publicly called unauthorized scraping a violation of creators' rights. Apple has not publicly responded. The case names five of the largest AI developers in the industry and was filed within the past three weeks, meaning this exposure category is accelerating in real time.

What was missing
No creator on YouTube had a mechanism to signal authorization state for AI training use in a machine-readable, queryable format. The only tools available were platform terms of service and robots.txt directives, both of which Apple allegedly circumvented. There was no consent record and no live authorization query layer.
What DDT changes
A DDT consent record embedded in or linked from content metadata gives any downstream system a queryable authorization signal before ingestion. A system that queries and receives REVOKED has a timestamped record of the signal. A system that bypasses the query has documented evidence of willful infringement. The Verification Receipt is the audit trail that separates negligent omission from willful conduct in discovery.
10
In re: OpenAI Copyright Litigation — NYT, Tribune, regional publishers Active
AI training · News publisher copyright · MDL, S.D.N.Y. · 16 consolidated cases · Discovery phase 2026
Pending
►

Sixteen copyright lawsuits from The New York Times, the Chicago Tribune, and regional publishers have been consolidated in the Southern District of New York. Judge Sidney Stein affirmed in January 2026 that OpenAI must produce 20 million ChatGPT output logs to plaintiffs, rejecting OpenAI's attempt to limit production to conversations that referenced plaintiff works specifically. The court found that output logs across all queries are discoverable because they bear on whether ChatGPT's outputs compete with or substitute for the original works. Legal analysts describe this ruling as the start of a discovery arms race in which AI companies must treat their data logs as high-velocity liabilities. The Debevoise firm predicts 2026 will bring sharper challenges to fair-use defenses, aggressive discovery into proprietary training data, and a new wave of class certification battles as plaintiff legal theories mature. Meanwhile, 23 major news organizations including the New York Times and USA Today have blocked the Wayback Machine's crawler entirely, destroying public archival records in an attempt to prevent further training data scraping. The publishers who blocked the archive did not have a consent layer. They had a drawbridge. DDT is the consent layer.

What was missing
News publishers had no machine-readable, live-queryable authorization signal for their content corpus. The absence of that signal meant the only available defense was blocking the crawler entirely, sacrificing three decades of public archival record in the process. In discovery, there is no consent record to produce. The governance audit trail the court is demanding does not exist.
What DDT changes
A publisher with DDT consent records can leave the archive open and enforce training authorization at the ingestion layer. The IPS query returns REVOKED for training use. The AI system has a timestamped record of the signal. The publisher has a timestamped record that the system was on notice. Both parties have the audit trail the S.D.N.Y. is demanding. The Verification Receipt is the governance document the court is ordering produced, generated automatically at the moment of attempted ingestion.

The attachment point question
Why this market does not need a decade to become insurable
Cyber — 1997
Attachment point: governance posture

No loss history existed. AIG set the attachment point on evidence of governance: firewalls, access controls, incident response plans. Imprecise. Manually verified. The market accepted it because nothing better existed. NotPetya in 2017 was when carriers finally understood systemic risk at scale.

AI identity — 2026
Attachment point: the consent record

A data breach closes. AI identity does not. A single moment of unconsented ingestion is permanent: the AI algorithm is altered, there is no surgical remedy, no restoration. The only defensible attachment point runs through a pre-ingestion consent record. Without it, the carrier is writing into open-ended, permanent exposure with no floor and no basis for a premium.

In 1997, the governance instrument was a firewall checklist. In 2026, it is DDT’s Verification Receipt.

Cryptographically signed. Timestamped. Machine-readable. Generated automatically at pre-ingestion. The floor is the receipt. The carrier writing coverage around it is not waiting for loss data to mature the market — it is setting the standard.

Why no existing product covers this

Five AI insurance products exist today: Google Cloud’s Risk Protection Program, Armilla at Lloyd’s, Testudo at Lloyd’s, AXA XL’s GenAI endorsement, and Munich Re’s aiSure.

Every one covers what happens after ingestion: model malfunction, output harm, performance failure.

Not one covers what happens before ingestion: whether the identity subject currently authorizes that specific use at that specific moment.
